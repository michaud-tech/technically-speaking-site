The scenario · 4 minutes

Here is what you know.

You should not have to invent half the situation just to practise the communication.

The idea

You are a TextNow intern working on an innovation challenge. Your team proposes an in-app feature that identifies signals associated with suspicious messages, explains why a message may be risky, and offers safer next steps.

The evidence

In interviews, nine of 14 users said they were sometimes unsure whether an unexpected message was legitimate. The other five did not report uncertainty. That does not prove they felt comfortable or trusted the message.

In a separate prototype test with different participants, six of eight noticed the warning and chose a safer action. The two results measure different things. They are not a before-and-after comparison.

The tradeoff

You want a four-week pilot using one product designer and two engineers. It would delay an onboarding experiment by one sprint. The pilot would test usage, comprehension, false warnings, and what longer-term measures are worth tracking.

Avery

Avery sponsors the innovation challenge. Avery can sponsor the pilot concept, but product and engineering leaders must approve the people and sprint time. Avery cares about user trust, speed to credible evidence, false warnings, and responsible use of limited design and engineering time.

Your job

Get Avery to sponsor the four-week pilot and help move it to product and engineering for resourcing.

T · Target Audience · Learn

You are talking to one person.

A room is made of people. Influence starts by understanding one human deeply, then scaling that thinking.

Macro: their world

What comes with their role: responsibilities, priorities, pressures, constraints, and the decisions they are accountable for.

Avery: user trust, credible evidence, limited product and engineering time.

Micro: this human, now

What matters in this conversation: motivations, concerns, decision style, words they repeat, and their current state.

Avery: worried a weak pilot could waste a sprint or create false alarms that erode trust.

Micro is not a personality test. It is situational awareness. You learn it by asking, observing, and checking your understanding.

Which one are you learning?

T · Target Audience · See it

Listening is how you find the real pitch.

You cannot align to Avery if you are only waiting for your turn to talk.

Tell me more

Invite depth without steering.

Let their language lead the next turn.

Paraphrase

Ask open questions

Worked example

Avery: “I need something credible enough to defend the sprint tradeoff.”

Weak response: “The prototype already proved it works.”

Stronger response: “It sounds like the question is not whether the idea is interesting. It is whether four weeks can produce evidence you can defend. Is that right?”

The stronger response does not surrender the idea. It checks whether you are solving the right problem.

E · End Goal · Learn

Know what you need to happen.

“Share an update” is not an end goal. The outcome that should follow the update is.

Action

A decision, commitment, approval, resource, or next step.

Understanding

Make sure you are actually seeing the tradeoff, risk, or new angle the same way.

Relational

Build trust, demonstrate capability, or deepen the working relationship.

Process

Create a new rhythm, owner, handoff, or way of working.

In this conversation

Primary goal, Action: Avery agrees to sponsor the pilot concept.

Supporting goal, Understanding: You and Avery agree on what the pilot must prove.

Second-best outcome: Avery names the missing evidence and agrees to a follow-up once you have it.

One conversation can affect all four. Choose the main outcome so you know what to drive toward.
E · End Goal · Practise

Ask the right person for the right thing.

Avery can sponsor the pilot. Avery cannot personally assign the designer and engineers. Your ask should match that authority.

Light, bright, tight

Light: only the context Avery needs to decide.

Bright: obvious what “yes” means.

Tight: stop after the ask and let Avery respond.

Worked example

“Avery, will you sponsor the four-week pilot and help us bring the resourcing request to product and engineering?”

That gives Avery one decision they can make and one next step they can help move.

Make one decision visible. Do not ask Avery to invent your next step.
E · End Goal · Live practice

A no is information.

An objection is a position. Your job is to understand the interest underneath it, then decide what the conversation needs.

1. Reflect

Name the content and, tentatively, the concern.

2. Check

Give Avery room to confirm or correct you.

3. Explore

If something is still vague, ask about it.

4. Respond

When you understand enough, offer a path through Avery's lens.

These are tools, not a script. You can combine moves. You can loop twice. You can stop. The goal is mutual understanding, not completing four steps in order.
Position and interest

Position: “We cannot spare the engineers.”

Possible interest: Avery may be protecting a deadline, the team’s credibility, or an onboarding result. Do not guess and solve. Check.

C · Clarity · Structure

Make the path easy to follow.

Clarity is not removing every detail. It is helping Avery see what this is, why it matters, what happens next, and when.

What

The idea or ask.

So what

Why it matters to Avery. This does most of the work.

Now what

The usable next step.

When

The timing, when timing matters.

TextNow example

What: We want to test the suspicious-message warning in a four-week pilot.

So what: It gives us stronger evidence about safer choices and false warnings before TextNow commits to a wider rollout.

Now what: Avery sponsors the concept, then we take the resourcing request to product and engineering.

When: We need the resourcing decision before the next sprint is planned.

C · Clarity · Metaphor

Make the impact familiar.

A metaphor translates the function of an unfamiliar idea into something Avery already understands.

The translation move

1
Name the core function.
What job does the idea do for the person who matters?
2
Find something familiar that does the same job.
Compare the function, not the shape or technology.
3
Return to the decision.
Explain why that comparison matters to Avery now.
Worked example

Core function: Give users a useful warning before they make a risky choice.

Familiar function: A dashboard warning light alerts a driver before a small issue becomes an expensive failure.

Metaphor: “This is closer to a dashboard warning light than a spam filter. It gives users a signal before they act. The pilot tells us whether the light is useful, or whether it flashes so often people stop trusting it.”

C · Clarity · Story

Move from pain to promise.

This story is about the change your idea could create. It begins with the user’s current experience and ends with the next credible step.

Worked TextNow example

Today: Users receive unexpected messages and sometimes cannot tell what is legitimate.

Every day: That uncertainty forces them to guess, and the wrong guess can expose them to harm.

Imagine if: They received a clear signal and a safer next step before they acted.

Because of that: More users could recognize risk in the moment, while we learn how often the system creates false warnings.

So that: TextNow can make a responsible decision about user trust using evidence instead of hope.

The ask: Sponsor the four-week pilot and help us take the resourcing request forward.

Keep the promise credible. A four-week pilot can produce better evidence. It cannot prove TextNow will become the world’s largest carrier.
C · Clarity · Revise

Use the tool that earns its place.

You tried structure, metaphor, and story. You do not need to cram all three into one pitch.

Structure

Best when the problem is order. Avery needs the path.

Metaphor

Best when the impact is hard to picture. Avery needs familiarity.

Story

Best when the stakes feel abstract. Avery needs to see the change.

H · How You Say It · Learn

Your voice tells people what matters.

Great content can still disappear if every sentence sounds the same. Delivery directs attention.

Pace

Slow down for an important number, conclusion, or ask. Speed up through familiar context.

Volume

Use contrast. A lift adds urgency. A quieter line draws people in.

Tone

Match the message: calm for facts, serious for risk, optimistic for a credible next step.

Pauses

Leave space before or after the line Avery needs to remember.

Mark the delivery

“Nine of fourteen users said they were sometimes unsure whether an unexpected message was legitimate. [pause] We have an early signal that a warning could help. [slow] We do not yet know whether it creates too many false alarms. [pause] That is what the pilot is for.”
