The TECH Model

TECH is a four-part model for getting your work understood and acted on. This mini-course gives you a chance to try it before we work together live.

Make a pitch, try the four parts of the model, then return to the pitch and try it again. Give yourself about 35–40 minutes, and have somewhere you can talk out loud.

T Target Audience
Know who you’re talking to, what matters to the business, and what matters to them.
E End Goal
Know what you want from the conversation and make a clear ask.
C Clarity
Make your point easy to follow and connect it to why it matters.
H How You Say It
Use pace, volume, tone and pauses to help your message land.

T · Target Audience · 2 min

What matters to them?

There’s what matters to the business, and there’s what matters to the person you’re talking to. Both shape what they need to hear.

A product team might want more people to finish setting up the app. Its engineering manager might be worried about fitting another change into a release that’s already full. The business result matters, and so does what that person has to manage.

Your turn: Now imagine you’re talking with Sam, who leads customer support. Sam is responsible for helping users get answers and managing the team’s day-to-day workload. You’re discussing how things have been going since the team started using a new support tool. Sam says:

“Our response time is better, but my team is exhausted. Every new tool seems to mean another place to check.”Sam, customer support lead

Think about what could matter to Sam personally and how it could affect the team or business. Take a stab in one or two sentences. You’re noticing possibilities, not making a pitch or deciding what Sam must be thinking.

Feedback connects to: Macro: Business and team · Micro: The person.

T · Target Audience · 3 min

Four listening tools

You don’t have to come up with your response while the other person is talking. Follow something they said and find out more.

“Our response time is better, but my team is exhausted. Every new tool seems to mean another place to check.”Sam, customer support lead · Example
Tell me more
Invite them to keep going on something that caught your attention.Tell me more about having another place to check.
Repeat the last few words
Echo a short phrase with curiosity, then leave space.Another place to check?
Paraphrase
Say what you understood in your own words so they can correct you.So responses are faster, but keeping up is wearing people out?
Open-ended questions
Ask something they can explain rather than answer with yes or no.What happens when the team has to check several places?

Your turn: Dani leads user research and says:

“We’re getting plenty of survey responses, but we keep hearing from the same users. I’m worried we’re missing the people who gave up on the app.”Dani, user research lead

Try a listening tool and see what Dani says. You can take up to three turns, then finish for a brief note on your listening.

Try one tool at a time. A short repeat-back is enough to invite more.

E · End Goal · 3 min

What are you asking for?

What do you want someone to think, feel or do after the conversation? Knowing that helps you decide what to say. Then make it easy for them to say yes or no.

Example

“Could you review this help article by Thursday? I need you to check the setup instructions before we publish it Friday.”

If Thursday won’t work: “Could we spend five minutes checking the setup steps now?” That’s a useful second-best outcome.

Your turn: You want ten minutes in Friday’s team meeting to test a new project handoff checklist. Lee runs the meeting and can give you the time.

Keep it to one useful alternative.

Feedback connects to: Clear goal · Clear ask + next step.

E · End Goal · 3 min

A no is information.

An objection is what someone is saying. Your job is to understand why they’re saying it. Then you can work out what to do next.

You’ve asked for a full rebuild of the app’s help center. Morgan replies:

“Can we try something smaller and cheaper before we commit to the whole rebuild?”
Reflect
Say back what you heard.You’d like a smaller test before we commit to the rebuild.
Check
Find out whether you understood.Is the concern spending the money before we know this will help?
Explore
Ask what’s behind the concern or what would help.What would you need to see from a smaller test?
Respond
Use what you’ve learned to offer a way forward.Let’s test one help topic before rebuilding the whole thing. Could we agree on what we need to learn?

You can move between these. You don’t need to fit all four into one response.

Your turn: You’ve asked Priya, an engineering lead, to try a new bug-report template with her team. She says:

“I can’t ask everyone to change how they report bugs in the middle of a release.”

Have a short conversation with Priya. Try reflecting, checking or exploring the concern before deciding how to respond. You can take up to three turns, then get notes on the whole exchange.

Feedback connects to: Clear goal · Clear ask + next step.

C · Clarity · 3 min

What / So What / Now What / When

Give the listener a way to follow you: what you’re talking about, why it matters, what should happen next and when. You can lead with the ask and then explain why. Use the order that makes the message easiest to follow.

Example

What: The app setup guide still shows last month’s screens.
So what: Support agents have to explain the differences to users instead of sending them a useful guide.
Now what: Could you approve an update to the screenshots?
When: Before Monday’s app update.

Your turn: Jo coordinates app testing. Three bug reports this week were missing the phone model, so engineers had to ask for it before they could investigate. You want Jo to add a phone-model field to the bug-report form for next week’s testing.

Cover What / So What / Now What / When in the order that works. You can combine parts.

Feedback connects to: Opening frame · Key evidence tied to impact.

C · Clarity · 3 min

Make it familiar

A metaphor uses something familiar to explain something unfamiliar. Choose a comparison the person you’re talking to will recognize, and show how it helps them understand your point.

Example

To a product manager who coaches football: “A project dashboard is like the scoreboard. It tells us whether we’re winning or losing. It doesn’t tell us every move to make.”

Your turn: Nina works in customer support and is learning how app updates reach users. She likes cooking. A staged rollout gives an update to a small group of users first, so the team can check for problems before more people receive it.

Use something from her world. Two sentences is plenty.

C · Clarity · 3 min

Pain to Promise

Start with a problem someone recognizes, show what could be different, and connect the change to something they care about.

Today… → Every day… → Imagine if… → Because of that… (three beats) → So that… → Ask

Example

Today, new team members hunt through old messages for setup instructions. Every day, someone has to explain the same steps again. Imagine if we had one current checklist. Because of that, new team members could find the steps themselves. Because of that, teammates could spend less time repeating instructions. Because of that, they could spend more time helping with the hard parts. So that new team members could get going with the support they actually need. Could we try one with the next new team member?

Your turn: Support agents keep sending the same login issue to different teams because nobody knows who owns it. Users wait while the report gets passed around. You want the support lead to try a shared list of issue owners for a week.

A short paragraph. Use the structure to connect the problem, the possible improvement and your ask.

H · How You Say It · 4 min

Same words. Different meaning.

Say each version out loud. Put the emphasis on the bold word.

  1. I didn’t say we should launch it today.
  2. I DIDN’T say we should launch it today.
  3. I didn’t SAY we should launch it today.
  4. I didn’t say WE should launch it today.
  5. I didn’t say we SHOULD launch it today.
  6. I didn’t say we should LAUNCH it today.
  7. I didn’t say we should launch IT today.
  8. I didn’t say we should launch it TODAY.

Emphasizing “it” might mean something else should launch today.

H · How You Say It · 3 min

Change the delivery

Pace
Slow down on the part you want people to catch.
Volume
Try a little louder or quieter to change the emphasis.
Tone
Say the same words with curiosity, concern or confidence.
Pauses
Leave space before or after the important part.
“I think we need another day to get this right.”

Say the sentence three ways: apologizing, as a calm recommendation, and with urgency. Try changing your pace, volume, tone and pauses.

Record all three in one take, leaving a pause between versions. Then listen back to compare them.
