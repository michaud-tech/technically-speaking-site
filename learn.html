Welcome · about 1 hour

Make your idea easier to understand—and act on.

TECH is a practical way to prepare for a conversation where you need someone to understand an idea, make a decision, or take action.

Start · Scenario · 4 minutes

Here is what you know.

Here’s the context you need to make a credible pitch.

The idea

You are a TextNow intern working on an innovation challenge. Your team proposes an in-app feature that identifies signals associated with suspicious messages, explains why a message may be risky, and offers safer next steps.

The evidence

In interviews, 9 of 14 users said they were sometimes unsure whether an unexpected message was legitimate. The other five did not report uncertainty. That does not prove they felt comfortable or trusted the message.

In a separate prototype test with different participants, 6 of 8 noticed the warning and chose a safer action. The two results measure different things. They are not a before-and-after comparison.

The tradeoff

You want a four-week pilot using one product designer and two engineers. It would delay an onboarding experiment by one sprint. The pilot would test usage, comprehension, false warnings, and what longer-term measures are worth tracking.

Avery

Avery sponsors the innovation challenge. Avery can sponsor the pilot concept, but product and engineering leaders must approve the people and sprint time. Avery cares about user trust, speed to credible evidence, false warnings, and responsible use of limited design and engineering time.

Your job

Get Avery to sponsor the four-week pilot and help move it to product and engineering for resourcing.

T · Target Audience · Learn

Imagine you’re only ever talking to one specific person at a time.

Decide who matters most and tailor the message to them. The more specific you are about one person’s needs, the more likely the message is to connect with everyone listening.

Start with what you know

Avery cares about user trust, credible evidence, false warnings, and responsible use of limited time. You can already emphasize those concerns.

Learn what would change the pitch

You still need Avery’s definition of credible evidence and what would make the sprint tradeoff defensible. Ask, listen, and adjust.

Your title is not your target audience. “Executive sponsor” gives you context. Listening tells you what this specific person needs from this specific conversation.
T · Target Audience · See it

Listening is how you find the real pitch.

You cannot align to Avery if you are only waiting for your turn to talk.

Tell me more

Invite depth without steering.

Repeat the last few words

Let their language lead the next turn.

Paraphrase

Say what you heard in your own words, then check it.

Ask open questions

Learn facts and the concern underneath them.

Worked example

Avery: “I need something credible enough to defend the sprint tradeoff.”

Weak response: “The prototype already proved it works.”

Stronger response: “It sounds like the question is not whether the idea is interesting. It is whether four weeks can produce evidence you can defend. Is that right?”

The stronger response does not surrender the idea. It checks whether you are solving the right problem.

E · End Goal · Learn

Decide what you want from the conversation.

Before you speak, know what you want the other person to understand, decide, or do. That is your end goal.

Action

A decision, commitment, approval, resource, or next step.

Understanding

Make sure you see a tradeoff, risk, or new angle the same way.

Relational

Build trust, demonstrate capability, or deepen the relationship.

Process

Create a new rhythm, owner, handoff, or way of working.

Choose one primary outcome. A conversation can create several benefits, but one outcome should guide what you say and what you ask for.

Example from another situation

Vague: “Priya, can you look over the groundwater results and let me know what you think?”

Clear: “Priya, will you approve four more weeks of groundwater monitoring before design sign-off?”

One decision can include its natural next step. Sponsoring this pilot can include championing the resource request with product and engineering.

E · End Goal · Live practice

A no is information.

An objection is a position. Your job is to understand the interest underneath it, then decide what the conversation needs.

1. Reflect

Name the content and, tentatively, the concern.

2. Check

Give Avery room to confirm or correct you.

3. Explore

If something is still vague, ask about it.

4. Respond

When you understand enough, offer a path through Avery's lens.

These are tools, not a script. You can combine moves. You can loop twice. You can stop. The goal is mutual understanding, not completing four steps in order.
Position and interest

Position: “The current evidence is not strong enough to justify delaying onboarding.”

Possible interest: Avery needs a pilot that can produce credible answers about behaviour, comprehension, and false warnings. Listen for the standard Avery needs, then respond to it.

C · Clarity · Tool 1 of 3

Try three ways to make the idea clear.

You’ll try What / So What / Now What / When, a metaphor, and Pain to Promise. Then you’ll choose the tool that helps Avery understand your idea best.

What

What are we talking about?

So what

Why does it matter?

Now what

What should happen next?

When

When will it happen, or when will you return to the decision?

TextNow example

What: I’m proposing a four-week pilot for the suspicious-message warning.

So what: It can give us credible evidence about immediate behaviour, comprehension, and false warnings before a larger investment.

Now what: I’m asking Avery to sponsor the pilot and champion the resource request.

When: I’ll send the detailed test plan Thursday at 3 p.m.

C · Clarity · Tool 2 of 3

Make the outcome familiar.

A useful metaphor helps Avery understand an unfamiliar outcome through a familiar comparison.

The translation move

1
Name the outcome the idea creates for the audience.
2
Think of something familiar that creates a similar outcome.
3
Connect the comparison back to your point.
Example from another situation

A new experiment dashboard helps a team see which tests need attention first. You might compare it to a departure board: it does not make the trip for you; it shows what is on time, what is delayed, and where to look next.

The comparison earns its place because it makes the outcome easier to picture.

C · Clarity · Tool 3 of 3

Move from pain to promise.

This story is about the change your idea could create. It begins with the user’s current experience and ends with the next credible step.

Example from another situation

Today: Warehouse supervisors discover equipment failures after work stops.

Every day: Teams lose time diagnosing preventable problems.

Imagine if: Supervisors saw early warning signs before a shutdown.

Because of that: A short trial could show which warnings help and which create noise.

So that: Operations can decide whether a broader rollout is worth the investment.

The ask: Approve a trial on one production line.

Keep the promise credible. A four-week pilot can produce better evidence. It cannot prove TextNow will become the world’s largest carrier.
