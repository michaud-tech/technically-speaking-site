The TECH Model

Make a pitch first. Try the four parts of TECH. Then return to the same pitch and see what changed.

This is a short primer before we work together live. Give yourself about 35 minutes, and have somewhere you can talk out loud.

T Target Audience
Know who you’re talking to, what matters to the business, and what matters to them.
E End Goal
Know what you want from the conversation and make a clear ask.
C Clarity
Make your point easy to follow and connect it to why it matters.
H How You Say It
Use pace, volume, tone and pauses to help your message land.

T · Target Audience · 2 min

What matters to them?

There’s what matters to the business, and there’s what matters to the person you’re talking to. Both shape what they need to hear.

A support team might need faster response times. But its manager might also be trying to keep an exhausted team from losing another person. If you only talk about speed, you can miss what matters to them.

“Our response time is better, but my team is exhausted. Every new tool seems to mean another place to check.”Sam, customer support lead

One or two sentences. Stay curious about the person.

T · Target Audience · 3 min

Four listening tools

You don’t have to come up with your response while the other person is talking. Follow something they said and find out more.

Tell me more
Invite them to keep going on something that caught your attention.Tell me more about having another place to check.
Repeat the last few words
Echo a short phrase with curiosity, then leave space.Another place to check?
Paraphrase
Say what you understood in your own words so they can correct you.So responses are faster, but keeping up is wearing people out?
Open-ended questions
Ask something they can explain rather than answer with yes or no.What happens when the team has to check several places?
“Our response time is better, but my team is exhausted. Every new tool seems to mean another place to check.”Sam

Try one tool in your own words. One response is enough.

E · End Goal · 3 min

What are you asking for?

What do you want someone to think, feel or do after the conversation? Knowing that helps you decide what to say. Then make it easy for them to say yes or no.

Example

“Could you review this draft by Thursday? I need your check on the customer examples before I send it Friday.”

If Thursday won’t work: “Could we spend five minutes on the two examples now?” That’s a useful second-best outcome.

Your turn: You want ten minutes in Friday’s team meeting to test a new project handoff checklist. Lee runs the meeting and can give you the time.

Keep it to one useful alternative.

Feedback connects to: E1: Clear goal · E2: Clear ask + next step.

E · End Goal · 3 min

A no is information.

An objection is what someone is saying. Your job is to understand why they’re saying it. Then you can work out what to do next.

You’ve asked for a full rebuild of an event registration page. Morgan replies:

“Can we try something smaller and cheaper before we commit to the whole rebuild?”
Reflect
Say back what you heard.You’d like a smaller test before we commit to the rebuild.
Check
Find out whether you understood.Is the concern spending the money before we know this will help?
Explore
Ask what’s behind the concern or what would help.What would you need to see from a smaller test?
Respond
Use what you’ve learned to offer a way forward.Let’s test the first page before rebuilding the whole thing. Could we agree on what we need to learn?

You can move between these. You don’t need to fit all four into one response.

One or two sentences. “What do you need from me to greenlight the project?” can be a useful question here too.

C · Clarity · 3 min

What / So What / Now What / When

Give the listener a way to follow you: what you’re talking about, why it matters, what should happen next and when.

Example

What: The printer is jamming every morning.
So what: Reception is keeping visitors waiting while they fix it.
Now what: Could you book a repair?
When: Before Monday’s open house.

Your turn: Jo runs a volunteer programme. Three volunteers missed shifts this month because schedule changes were buried in a group chat. You want Jo to try a shared calendar for next week’s shifts.

C · Clarity · 3 min

Make it familiar

A metaphor uses something familiar to explain something unfamiliar. Choose a comparison the person you’re talking to will recognise, and show how it helps them understand your point.

Example

To a football coach: “A project dashboard is like the scoreboard. It shows where we stand, so we know what needs attention. It doesn’t tell us every move to make.”

Your turn: Nina runs a busy community kitchen. She’s never used a password manager. It stores different passwords so people don’t have to remember or reuse one password everywhere.

Use something from her world. Two sentences is plenty. A password manager doesn’t guarantee security.

C · Clarity · 3 min

Pain to Promise

Start with a problem someone recognises, show what could be different, and connect the change to something they care about.

Today… → Every day… → Imagine if… → Because of that… → So that… → Ask

Example

Today, new starters hunt through old messages for setup instructions. Every day, someone has to explain the same steps again. Imagine if we had one current checklist. Because of that, people could find the steps themselves, so the team could spend more time helping with the hard parts. Could we try one with the next new starter?

Your turn: Students arrive at the equipment cupboard and find cameras missing. The lab coordinator spends time tracking them down. You want to try a sign-out sheet for a week.

A short paragraph. Use the structure to connect the problem, the possible improvement and your ask.

H · How You Say It · 4 min

Same words. Different meaning.

Say each version out loud. Put the emphasis on the bold word.

  1. I didn’t say we should launch it today.
  2. I DIDN’T say we should launch it today.
  3. I didn’t SAY we should launch it today.
  4. I didn’t say WE should launch it today.
  5. I didn’t say we SHOULD launch it today.
  6. I didn’t say we should LAUNCH it today.
  7. I didn’t say we should launch IT today.
  8. I didn’t say we should launch it TODAY.

Emphasising “it” might mean something else should launch today.

H · How You Say It · 3 min

Change the delivery

Pace
Slow down on the part you want people to catch.
Volume
Try a little louder or quieter to change the emphasis.
Tone
Say the same words with curiosity, concern or confidence.
Pauses
Leave space before or after the important part.
“I think we need another day to get this right.”

Say it once as if you’re apologising. Say it again as a calm recommendation. Deliberately change your pace, volume, tone or pauses, then listen to both.
