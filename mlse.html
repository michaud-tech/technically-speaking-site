The TECH Model

TECH is a four-part model for getting your ideas understood and acted on. Here you’ll use it on inbound client conversations: working out what the client actually needs, balancing what MLSE can deliver, and finding a way forward, even when the answer to the original request is no.

Make a pitch, try the four parts of the model, then return to the pitch and try it again. Give yourself about 35–40 minutes, and have somewhere you can talk out loud.

T Target Audience
Know who you’re talking to, what matters to the business, and what matters to them.
E End Goal
Ask clearly for what you want, and know how you’ll handle pushback.
C Clarity
Use stories and metaphors to make complex work easy to grasp and its impact on your audience obvious.
H How You Say It
Use pace, volume, tone, and pauses to make the important parts land.

These practice situations are fictional. The clients, requests, availability and internal constraints are invented for training, not MLSE policy or inventory. No sign-in needed. T · Target Audience · 2 min

What matters to them?

There’s what matters to the business, and there’s what matters to the person you’re talking to. Both shape what they need to hear.

A client’s company might want more pipeline from its events. The marketing director you’re talking to might be worried about walking back into a VP meeting with an idea that’s already been turned down. The business result matters, and so does what that person has to manage.

Your turn: Sam, a marketing lead at a consumer brand, has reached out to you about a partnership. Sam answers to a CEO and works closely with a sales team that sells into retailers. On your first call, Sam says:

“We need something big with the Leafs. Our CEO wants visibility, sales wants retailer meetings, and we have to spend the budget this quarter.”Sam, client marketing lead

Think about what could matter to Sam personally and what Sam’s business needs. Take a stab in one or two sentences. You’re noticing possibilities, not proposing a package or deciding what Sam must be thinking.

Feedback connects to: Macro: Business and team · Micro: The person.

T · Target Audience · 3 min

Four listening tools

You don’t have to come up with your response while the other person is talking. Follow something they said and find out more.

“The last event looked great, but our sales team said it was a waste of their night.”Alex, client marketing director · Example
Tell me more
Invite them to keep going on something that caught your attention.Tell me more about why it felt like a waste.
Repeat the last few words
Echo a short phrase with curiosity, then leave space.A waste of their night?
Paraphrase
Say what you understood in your own words so they can correct you.So it looked good, but it didn’t give sales what they needed?
Open-ended questions
Ask something they can explain rather than answer with yes or no.What would have made it worth their night?

Your turn: Dani, a marketing director at a business-services company, makes an inbound request:

“Can you get us a suite and a player? We need our top prospects to actually show up this time.”Dani, client marketing director

Nothing is confirmed yet, so don’t pitch a package. Try a listening tool and find out what Dani actually needs. You can take up to three turns, then finish for a brief note on your listening.

Try one tool at a time. A short repeat-back is enough to invite more.

E · End Goal · 3 min

What are you asking for?

What do you want someone to think, feel or do after the conversation? Knowing that helps you decide what to say. Then make it easy for them to say yes or no.

Example

“Could you send me your top three priorities for the event by Wednesday? I need them to check what’s possible with our events team before your Friday meeting.”

If Wednesday won’t work: “Could we take five minutes now to rank the must-haves?” That’s a useful second-best outcome.

Your turn: Sometimes the ask is internal. Your client needs an answer Thursday on whether a hosted pre-game reception for 30 guests is possible. Lee leads event delivery, and their team is focused on contracted partner events this month. You need 15 minutes with Lee by Wednesday to check feasibility.

Keep it to one useful alternative.

Feedback connects to: Clear goal · Clear ask + next step.

E · End Goal · 3 min

A no is information.

An objection is what someone is saying. Your job is to understand why they’re saying it. Then you can work out what to do next.

You’ve told Morgan, a client, that the branded activation they wanted is already committed to another partner. Morgan replies:

“If we can’t have the activation, I’m not sure this is worth it for us.”
Reflect
Say back what you heard.The activation was a big part of why this felt worth doing.
Check
Find out whether you understood.Is the concern that without it, your brand won’t stand out?
Explore
Ask what’s behind the concern or what would help.What did you need the activation to do for you?
Respond
Use what you’ve learned to offer a way forward.If the goal is getting noticed by those buyers, there may be other ways to do that. Could I check two options and come back to you Thursday?

You can move between these. You don’t need to fit all four into one response. And sometimes the honest answer is that nothing fits right now. That’s still a useful outcome.

Your turn: Priya, a client marketing director, asked for a player-led client event in four weeks. You’ve confirmed a player isn’t available in that window. A hospitality-led event might work, but capacity, cost and rights still need checking. Priya says:

“Without the player, this is just an expensive night out. I’ve already taken the idea to my VP.”

Have a short conversation with Priya. Try reflecting, checking or exploring the concern before deciding how to respond. The player is a confirmed no, so don’t reopen it. You can take up to three turns, then get notes on the whole exchange.

Feedback connects to: Clear goal · Clear ask + next step.

C · Clarity · 3 min

What / So What / Now What / When

Give the listener a way to follow you: what you’re talking about, why it matters, what should happen next and when. You can lead with the ask and then explain why. Use the order that makes the message easiest to follow.

Example

What: The branded activation you asked for isn’t available on that date.
So what: It’s already committed to another partner, so I don’t want you building plans around it.
Now what: Could we look at whether a hosted pre-game reception would meet your goal of meeting buyers?
When: I’ll come back to you by Thursday with what’s possible.

Your turn: Jo, a client at a retail brand, asked for a custom concourse activation in three weeks. Your delivery team has confirmed it can’t be built safely in that time. Jo’s real goal is product conversations with invited trade customers before a buying meeting. A hosted discussion in an existing space might work, but space, cost and rights aren’t confirmed. You can update Jo by Wednesday.

Cover What / So What / Now What / When in the order that works. You can combine parts. Don’t promise the alternative is available.

C · Clarity · 3 min

Make it familiar

A metaphor uses something familiar to explain something unfamiliar. Choose a comparison the person you’re talking to will recognize, and show how it helps them understand your point.

Example

To a client VP who coaches minor hockey: “A partnership proposal is like a game plan. It shows how we intend to win. It doesn’t guarantee the score.”

Your turn: Nina, a client marketing director, runs her company’s conferences. She asks why moving her activation to a different MLSE venue wouldn’t automatically make it available. Each option still needs its own check on rights, inventory and delivery capacity.

Use something from her world. Two sentences is plenty.

C · Clarity · 3 min

Pain to Promise

Start with a problem someone recognizes, show what could be different, and connect the change to something they care about.

Today… → Every day… → Imagine if… → Because of that… (three beats) → So that… → Ask

Example

Today, your team invites a big crowd to every game night. Every time, sales gets a few good conversations and misses the buyers they came for. Imagine if the night was built around twenty of the right people. Because of that, your reps could prepare for each conversation. Because of that, guests would get real time with your team. Because of that, those conversations could turn into next meetings. So that your event budget goes toward the pipeline you actually need. Could we look at what a smaller hosted night would involve?

Your turn: A business-services client asks for a big fan-facing activation. Their last event drew a crowd, but sales had little time with the buyers they needed. You want to recommend exploring a smaller invited-customer experience with planned discussion time. It means less exposure, nothing is guaranteed, and feasibility isn’t confirmed yet.

A short paragraph. Use the structure to connect the problem, the possible improvement, the tradeoff and your ask.

H · How You Say It · 4 min

Same words. Different meaning.

Say each version out loud. Put the emphasis on the bold word.

  1. I didn’t say we can’t do it this season.
  2. I DIDN’T say we can’t do it this season.
  3. I didn’t SAY we can’t do it this season.
  4. I didn’t say WE can’t do it this season.
  5. I didn’t say we CAN’T do it this season.
  6. I didn’t say we can’t DO it this season.
  7. I didn’t say we can’t do IT this season.
  8. I didn’t say we can’t do it THIS SEASON.

Emphasizing “we” might mean someone else can’t do it.

H · How You Say It · 3 min

Change the delivery

Pace
Slow down on the part you want people to catch.
Volume
Try a little louder or quieter to change the emphasis.
Tone
Say the same words with curiosity, concern or confidence.
Pauses
Leave space before or after the important part.
“We can’t make the player appearance work, but I think there’s another way to get you there.”

Say the sentence three ways: apologizing, defensively, and as a calm recommendation. Try changing your pace, volume, tone and pauses.
